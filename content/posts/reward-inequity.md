Many animals exchange goods and services similar to the ways in which humans trade. These exchanges in so-called ‘biological markets’ can be relatively simple: you scratch my back, I scratch yours, literally. Individuals often exchange grooming, especially in those hard to reach areas where only a friend can get to. But sometimes exchanges can be between individuals of different species, or involve very different behaviours. For example, when a ray is cleaned by a small cleaner fish, the ray receives a hygienic service and the cleaner fish receives a meal. Because of this trade, the laws of supply demand apply: when there are more rays (high demand for cleaners), each ray receives a lesser service because the cleaner can just get another ray if this one leaves; when there are more cleaner fish (high supply of cleaners), rays can be demand better treatment or they will go to a competitor, meaning that cleaners need to up their game to compete with others.

While biological markets are often studied in the context of these amazing interspecies encounters involving two individuals, the behaviours that individuals trade can sometime have an effect on multiple individuals or a whole group. These behaviours that benefit whole groups – public goods – pose a problem for the classical biological markets model. Biological markets are maintained by individuals’ ability to choose who they provide their service to (like the rays choosing the best cleaners to provide a meal to). But when an individual produces something that is beneficial to everyone nearby, then it loses its ability to choose who benefits from its public service. This means that this helpful individual opens itself up to exploitation – having received the benefit of the producer’s behaviour already, why should any individual pay for that service? And whose responsibility is it is to pay if everybody benefits?

This is precisely the situation that dwarf mongooses find themselves in. Dwarf mongooses are small group-living mammals that spend most of their days in the African bush with their heads in the ground, digging for food. While this is the only way for them to get a decent meal, digging leaves the mongooses vulnerable to predation. To get around this, the group often has one individual on sentinel duty. This individual will perch somewhere high up and watch for predators, foregoing their own foraging needs but keeping the others safe. If a predator does come along, the sentinel will make an alarm call, allowing everyone to get to safety. Because the others in the group know that someone is watching their back, they can spend more time foraging and less time looking up and around for predators, meaning that they can get more food when a sentinel is on duty. This is the public good.

But what do sentinels receive in return for guarding the group? In the dwarf mongooses, there is observational and experimental evidence that individuals who spend more time on sentinel duty also receive more grooming, which groups always do when they get back to their burrow after a day of foraging. Previous work shows that the more an individual is on sentinel duty during the day, the more grooming it receives. But we don’t know the relationship between how much grooming an individual receives and how much sentinel effort it will to put in the next day. Does the reward that an individual receives for its public service affect how much it is willing to contribute to the public good?

We investigated this question using an N-player game-theoretic model. In this model, individuals could contribute to a public good and receive rewards accordingly. But we changed how individuals received rewards. Specifically, we changed how fairly individuals were rewarded for their public good contribution. Are rewards proportional to the contribution (thus ‘equitable’)? Or do some individuals receive much more reward than others for just a little bit more effort (i.e., rewards are inequitable)?
When we manipulate this, our model predicts – counterintuitively – that when rewards are inequitable, individuals make (on average) greater contributions to the public good than when rewards are equitable. So unfairness in reward leads to greater effort. But why is this?

In our model, an individual’s reward is always related to how much of a contribution it makes. This means that individuals who make a greater contribution always receive more reward, and the inequity determines how much more reward they get for a small amount more effort. This means that an individual who is making slightly less contribution than others can receive much less reward. Thinking of it another way, an individual can improve its reward a lot by just contributing that little bit more than others. If all individuals do this though, this competition pushes up the contribution that everyone has to make to get that higher reward. This is why inequitable rewards can result in higher contributions to public goods, because it induces competition amongst would-be contributors.

So inequity is a good thing, then?

Well, no.

The higher contribution by some individuals is not the whole story. Inequitable rewards also result in free riders, who give a much lower effort than others. This is because when there is a lot of contribution to the public good (for example, lots of individuals are willing to sentinel and watch for predators), then some individuals do better to avoid the costs of contributing. Sure, they also miss out on the reward but as long as they receive the benefits from others’ public-goods contributions then they’re better off not putting the effort in.

Our model is applicable to scenarios in which individuals are rewarded for their contributions to public goods. This includes sentinel behaviour in the dwarf mongooses, as well as male vervet monkeys being rewarded with grooming by females for participating in intergroup contests. There is no reason why our model couldn’t apply to some human scenarios, but we caution against over-interpreting the results with application to humans, and we are definitely not arguing that inequity amongst people is something we aim for (obviously…). There are many other factors at play that might affect how such scenarios as we modelled actually play out in nature. The main one being that we didn’t model how the rewarders might adjust their behaviour in response to changing contributions to public goods. While our current model tells us how contributors might respond to different types of reward, investigating the coevolution of rewarders will give us a better idea of what we might expect to see in nature.
