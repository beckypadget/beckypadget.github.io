Mongoose markets: How rewards can promote public goods production (and free riding)

By Rebecca Padget Posted on 2026-05-20

Photo by Audric Wonkam on Unsplash

Rewards for public goods

Animals in groups often contribute to the production of benefits that impact their whole group, so-called ‘public goods’. These public goods can be really important, providing early warning systems against predators, territory defence or resource acquisition, so you might expect that individuals are willing to contribute. However, an individual actually does best of all if it doesn’t have to contribute but can instead rely on others’ contributions. If enough individuals do this, though, then the public good might not be produced at all and everyone loses out.

To avoid this, some animals appear to give rewards to individuals who participate in the production of public goods. This type of ‘payment’ or reward for public goods contributions is likely to encourage others to contribute, ensuring that everybody benefits. What we don’t know is the extent to which this reward can promote contributions or whether it matters exactly how rewards are allocated. So we modelled it.

Dwarf mongoose sentinels

Our model was inspired by dwarf mongoose sentineling. Dwarf mongooses are small group-living mammals that live throughout eastern Africa and while they’re out and about foraging during the day, they often have a sentinel. The sentinel finds a raised perch and watches to for predators, giving an alarm call if they spot danger. This allows everyone else in the group to concentrate on their foraging, even though the sentinel itself cannot forage while on duty. Apparently as a reward for their efforts, sentinels receive more grooming when the group returns home to the sleeping burrow at the end of the day (Kern & Radford, 2018).

The model

We developed a mathematical model inspired by the mongooses and based around principles of game theory in which contributors to a public good are rewarded by others. We used this model to investigate how public goods production might differ depending on how rewards are allocated. Rewards could be allocated either proportionally to an individual’s public goods contribution or disproportionately. If rewards are allocated proportionally, this means that an individual who contributes, for example, 10% more to the public good than others receives 10% more reward. If rewards are allocated disproportionately, an individual contributing 10% more than others in the group could receive all of the reward, with others getting nothing, even if they contributed a bit.

What we found

When rewards are allocated disproportionately, individuals have to meet a threshold contribution before they are rewarded but what this threshold actually is depends on how much others are contributing (more contribution = higher threshold). This means that individuals who are contributing just below this threshold might have a lot of reward to gain by increasing their contribution just slightly. When we get a group of individuals doing this, it pushes up the contributions that they make. This means that disproportionate rewards can result in higher contributions to the public good.

But this isn’t the whole story. As that threshold contribution required before rewards are received is pushed up, it becomes harder for individuals to meet this threshold. This is because there are costs to contributing (for example, an individual on sentinel duty cannot forage), and these costs increase the greater the contribution is. So for some individuals, when the threshold is high, it is better to forego any chance of reward by not contributing because they then don’t have to pay the costs. Because of this, disproportionate rewards also encourage some individuals to ‘free ride’.

What does this mean?

Our model predicts not just that rewards can promote contributions to public goods (previous work already showed this), but that the way in which these rewards are given matters.

More broadly, our model lets us think about public goods production as a behaviour that can be traded (e.g., for a reward). This potentially opens up some new ways of thinking about and modelling public goods behaviours as commodities in a market, which could be a powerful framework for thinking about how such behaviours evolve.
